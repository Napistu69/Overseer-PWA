## The Discovery Mechanism

An egg overcooked in a pot. The yolk boundary turns grey-green. The compound responsible is iron(II) sulfide — FeS — forming through a precise reaction where two separate chemistries converge at a single boundary:

On the white side: albumen is rich in sulfur-bearing amino acids, chiefly **cysteine** and **methionine**. Sustained heat decomposes these, releasing **hydrogen sulfide gas (H₂S)**. The outer white heats first and hottest, so H₂S diffuses inward toward the yolk.

On the yolk side: iron is concentrated, largely bound in a phosphoprotein called **phosvitin**. Prolonged heating mobilizes that iron as **ferrous ions (Fe²⁺)**, which leach outward toward the white.

Where they meet:

> Fe²⁺ + H₂S → FeS + 2H⁺

Iron sulfide. Gray-green. Deposited as a thin layer precisely at the yolk surface — the exact point where white meets yellow. A boundary phenomenon. Two flows converging from opposite directions, reacting at the interface.

The reaction is controlled by four variables: cooking time (longer equals more H₂S generated and more iron released), temperature (higher equals faster amino acid decomposition), cooling speed (slow cooling lets the reaction continue after heat removal; rapid chilling halts it), and egg age (older eggs develop higher pH whites — rising from approximately 7.6 to 9.3 over weeks — which accelerates H₂S production significantly).

The gut recognizes this chemistry. Eat that egg and the body responds: bloating, gas, microbial fermentation audible as a rising internal hum. The same sulfur compounds, the same anaerobic bacteria, the same gas production — sulfate-reducers doing what sulfate-reducers have done for billions of years.
