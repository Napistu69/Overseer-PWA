## II. THE D'ARCY CONCESSION (1901)

### The Contract That Built the Modern World

On May 28, 1901, William Knox D'Arcy — a London-born mining magnate — signed a concession agreement with Mozaffar al-Din Shah of Persia (Qajar dynasty). The terms granted D'Arcy exclusive rights to explore, extract, and transport petroleum, natural gas, and asphalt across the entirety of the Persian Empire for a period of sixty years.

The consideration: £20,000 cash, £20,000 in paid-up shares, and 16% of net profits.

The Shah needed money. His treasury was depleted by royal extravagance and foreign loans. D'Arcy needed a frontier. His Australian gold mining ventures had made him wealthy, but the gold was running out. He needed the next resource — the one that would define the century.

Neither party understood what they had signed. D'Arcy did not know if oil existed in commercial quantities in Persia. The Shah did not know that the substance beneath his soil would redraw every border, topple every dynasty, and militarize every trade route on Earth within fifty years.

### The Exploration Years (1901–1908)

D'Arcy dispatched geologists to Persia almost immediately. The conditions were brutal: 120-degree heat, hostile terrain, tribal resistance, Ottoman border skirmishes, and the complete absence of infrastructure. By 1903, D'Arcy's personal fortune was nearly exhausted. By 1905, he was seeking additional investors to keep the concession alive.

The British Admiralty entered the picture. First Sea Lord John Fisher had been advocating since 1882 for the conversion of the Royal Navy from coal to oil. The strategic rationale: oil-powered ships were faster, required less crew for fuel handling, could refuel at sea, and did not need to stop frequently for coaling. A navy running on oil could project power globally without the constraint of coaling stations.

But Britain had no domestic oil. Coal was abundant; oil was not. A navy dependent on oil would be a navy dependent on foreign supply — a strategic vulnerability unless the supply was under British control.

D'Arcy's Persian concession was the answer.

### The First Strike (1908)

On May 26, 1908 — twenty-six days short of the concession's seventh anniversary — drilling at Masjed-e-Soleiman struck oil at 1,180 feet. The gusher was enormous. Commercial viability was confirmed.

Within a year, D'Arcy's concession was restructured into the Anglo-Persian Oil Company (APOC), incorporated on April 14, 1909. The British government, through the Admiralty, had quietly ensured that British capital controlled the venture.

### The Sixty-Year Clock Begins

1901 => concession signed

1951 => Iran nationalizes oil; Mossadegh coup follows (1953)

Sixty years. One contract. One resource. One arc from desert concession to presidential warning.

## III. THE APOC NEXUS — OIL, NAVY, AND STATE

### The 1914 Government Acquisition

The rationale was strategic: the Royal Navy's conversion to oil depended on a secure supply, and secure supply meant ownership, not merely contract.

Churchill's argument to Parliament: "We must become the owners, or at any rate the controllers, at the source of at least a proportion of the oil which we require... and obtain our oil supply, so far as possible, from sources under British control."

Parliament agreed. The Anglo-Persian Oil Company Act passed. The British state and the oil company became the same entity.
